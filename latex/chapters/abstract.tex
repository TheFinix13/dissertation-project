A pension fund manager has two obligations at once. The first is to not lose more than a stated percentage of the fund's value from its recent peak; this is the drawdown limit written into the fund's mandate. The second is to earn a return above cash. Standard quantitative tools force a trade-off between the two. Mean-variance optimisation~\parencite{markowitz1952portfolio} is single-period and blind to the running peak. Trailing stop-losses fire only after the drawdown has already happened. Value-at-Risk~\parencite{rockafellar2000optimization} measures one bad day at a time and is silent on the path of the equity curve.

This dissertation studies whether a small machine-learning agent can sit on a more attractive point of that trade-off. The agent is a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) policy~\parencite{schulman2017ppo}. PPO is the standard policy-gradient algorithm for sequential decision problems where today's action affects tomorrow's state. The agent is paired with a separate forecaster: a probabilistic Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network~\parencite{hochreiter1997lstm,salinas2020deepar} that emits not just a prediction of tomorrow's return but a confidence interval around that prediction. The width of that interval is fed to the policy as an uncertainty score.

The uncertainty score enters the policy in two places. It is added as one coordinate of the policy's state, so the policy can condition on it. It is also used as a hard guard, so that new buys are blocked when the score exceeds a quantile threshold. The mathematical contribution is one extension to the standard trade-size computation in the trading environment; the formal statement is in Section~\ref{sec:env_and_contribution}.

The agent is compared against three named alternatives on the same protocol: passive buy-and-hold of the underlying index, a manually-tuned trailing stop-loss rule, and a baseline PPO that sees no uncertainty signal. The protocol fixes the train, validation and test splits, the random seeds, and the metric set so that all four agents are evaluated like-for-like.

The headline result is the full experimental grid. Every agent variant is trained on a market sample of 70 stocks, with ten random starting points per stock and fifty thousand training rounds per cell. On the held-out 2022--2025 test window, the uncertainty-aware agent grows a \$1\,million starting portfolio to a median final value of about \$1.6\,million. Its median Sharpe ratio is $+0.68$ to $+0.70$ and its win-rate is 89--92\,\% --- the fraction of stocks on which the agent finishes with more money than it started. The baseline PPO, which sees no uncertainty signal, finishes essentially flat at a median of about \$999\,000, with a median Sharpe of $-0.04$ and a win-rate of 46\,\%. A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 70 paired tickers rejects the null at $p < 10^{-12}$ for terminal value, Sharpe, and maximum drawdown. The 95\,\% paired-bootstrap confidence interval for the uncertainty-aware agent's terminal-value gain over the baseline is \$559k to \$814k.

The advantage holds out of sample. A four-fold walk-forward validation trains the agent on an earlier window and tests it on a strictly later one. The four test windows together span 2018--2025 and cover the COVID crash, the 2022 inflation shock, and the 2024--2025 recovery. The uncertainty-aware agent wins on every fold. The overall win-rate is 87\,\% and the median Sharpe is $+0.55$ (paired Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-8}$).

A third experimental arm was added in response to the project-proposal feedback. The same forecaster is also evaluated in an epistemic-uncertainty mode using Monte-Carlo dropout~\parencite{gal2016dropout}, which measures the model's unfamiliarity with today's data rather than the noise in the data itself. The two arms are close: aleatoric and epistemic differ by \$8.6k (95\,\% CI $-\$22.8$k to $+\$4.0$k, not significant) in median terminal value across the grid. They differ by a statistically detectable but small $+0.014$ in median Sharpe (in favour of aleatoric, $p = 7 \times 10^{-4}$). The practical implication is that any calibrated uncertainty signal helps; the specific source is a small effect.

The contribution is a controlled empirical study, not a new algorithm. What is new is the four-agent comparison on a single reproducible protocol with the trailing-stop comparator that most of the deep-reinforcement-learning literature on finance leaves out, and the public, runnable end-to-end Jupyter walkthrough that reproduces it.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} reinforcement learning; uncertainty quantification; drawdown control; portfolio risk management; walk-forward validation.
