\section{Risk-management objectives in portfolio practice}
\label{sec:risk_measures}

Most people think about investment risk as volatility --- how much a price wiggles up and down day to day. Institutions think about something different: \emph{drawdown}, the fractional fall in portfolio value from its running peak. Imagine a portfolio starts at one million dollars, climbs to 1.5 million, and then falls back to 1.2 million. Its drawdown at that point is twenty per cent, measured against the 1.5 million peak rather than against the starting balance. The distinction matters because institutional mandates are written in drawdown terms, not volatility terms~\parencite{calpers2022policy,dalio2017principles}, and the four families of risk measures reviewed below differ on whether they can express that constraint at all.

\subsection{Mean-variance and its tradition}
\label{sec:markowitz}

\textcite{markowitz1952portfolio} formalised portfolio choice as a quadratic optimisation in the mean and variance of returns (Development; the framework defines the optimisation backbone Chapter~\ref{ch:results} compares against). Given $N$ assets with vector of expected returns $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and return covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$, the minimum-variance portfolio for a target expected return $\mu^\star$ solves
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:markowitz}
  \min_{\vect{w}} \; \tfrac{1}{2} \vect{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \vect{w}
  \quad \text{subject to} \quad
  \vect{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu} = \mu^\star, \quad \vect{w}^\top \mathbf{1} = 1,
\end{equation}
where $\vect{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the vector of portfolio weights and $\mathbf{1}$ is the vector of ones.

The framework is single-period: it cannot tell the difference between a smooth ten-per-cent loss and a twenty-five-per-cent drawdown that recovers to a ten-per-cent loss by the rebalancing date, even though only the second breaches an institutional drawdown limit. The estimation of $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ on a finite training window also produces weights that are notoriously sensitive to noise~\parencite{michaud1989markowitz} (Positioning; this is the empirical-stability critique the dissertation's evaluation protocol respects by holding splits and seeds fixed). \textcite{black1992global} address the estimation problem by anchoring $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ to market-implied returns (Positioning; an alternative response to the same noise problem). \textcite{demiguel2009naive} show that an equal-weight portfolio frequently outperforms estimation-heavy alternatives out of sample (Evaluation; provides the naive benchmark a portfolio-control study should beat). \textcite{maillard2010risk} introduce risk parity, which equalises the contribution of each asset to portfolio variance (Positioning; a third response in the static-optimisation tradition).

\subsection{Tail-risk measures}
\label{sec:var_es}

\textcite{rockafellar2000optimization} summarise risk not by the second moment but by the tail of the loss distribution. Value-at-Risk at confidence level $\alpha$ is the loss exceeded with probability at most $1 - \alpha$, and expected shortfall (also called conditional Value-at-Risk) is the average loss in that tail:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:var}
  \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \inf \{ \ell \in \mathbb{R} : P(L > \ell) \leq 1 - \alpha \},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:es}
  \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = \mathbb{E}[L \mid L \geq \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)].
\end{equation}
\textcite{artzner1999coherent} show that expected shortfall is coherent (subadditivity, monotonicity, positive homogeneity, translation invariance). Both measures are single-period and silent on the path of the equity curve. The dissertation reports the VaR-95 violation rate (Evaluation) as part of the standard metric set in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol} but its headline objective is path-dependent.

\subsection{Path-dependent measures: drawdown}
\label{sec:drawdown}

The drawdown family of risk measures matches institutional mandates directly. The maximum drawdown of a portfolio with value process $V_0, V_1, \ldots, V_T$ is the worst fractional loss from the running peak:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mdd}
  \mathrm{MDD}_T = \max_{t \in [0, T]} \left( 1 - \frac{V_t}{\max_{s \leq t} V_s} \right).
\end{equation}
\textcite{magdon2004sample} derive a closed-form expected maximum drawdown for a Brownian-motion price model (Positioning; the analytical baseline against which empirical MDDs are read). \textcite{young1991calmar} introduce the Calmar ratio (annualised return divided by MDD) as the practitioner-facing return-per-unit-drawdown measure. \textcite{chekhlov2005drawdown} generalise MDD from a single-event statistic to a tail-expectation over the full drawdown distribution (Conditional Drawdown-at-Risk; Positioning, the path-dependent analogue of expected shortfall). The capital-preservation ratio of Equation~\ref{eq:preservation_ratio} is a complement of one over the drawdown family.

\subsection{Downside deviation: Sortino}
\label{sec:sortino}

\textcite{sortino1994downside} replace the symmetric standard deviation in the Sharpe ratio's denominator with the downside deviation. The Sortino ratio addresses the criticism that mean-variance penalises upside variability as much as downside, but it is still per-period and does not bind a drawdown limit.

\subsection{Behavioural roots of the drawdown constraint}
\label{sec:behavioural}

\textcite{kahneman1979prospect} introduced prospect theory; \textcite{tversky1992cumulative} extended it to cumulative form and estimated that losses are felt roughly twice as painfully as equivalent gains (Positioning; explains why retail clients react to drawdown rather than to variance and why a drawdown-aware policy has a behavioural payoff beyond the institutional-mandate payoff).

\subsection{Synthesis}
\label{sec:risk_synthesis}

The families differ on two axes: \emph{single-period vs path-dependent} (the path-dependent family is just the drawdown one), and \emph{static optimisation vs dynamic policy} (the static-optimisation tradition picks one weight vector and sticks with it). This dissertation occupies the intersection: a path-dependent objective enforced through a dynamic policy. Section~\ref{sec:problem_formulation} states the constrained objective formally.

\section{Reactive-rule risk control}
\label{sec:reactive_rules}

Practitioner risk control is dominated not by mean-variance recalibration but by reactive rules: stop-loss orders, trailing stops, position-size caps, and Kelly-style sizing~\parencite{kelly1956new} (Positioning; this is the second tradition the dissertation's reinforcement-learning candidate is compared against, and the trailing-stop comparator of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is the explicit operationalisation of it). The textbook trailing stop sells the position when the value falls more than $k$\,\% below the running peak and re-enters when the price rises above a moving average. The rule is easy to implement and is operationally common; it is also reactive --- the stop fires after the drawdown has already happened. The dissertation's results (Section~\ref{sec:headline_aggregate}) show two trailing-stop variants finishing below passive buy-and-hold on the same test window, including path drawdowns larger than buy-and-hold's, because the stop sells into the dip and the moving-average re-entry forfeits the recovery.

\section{Reinforcement learning fundamentals}
\label{sec:rl_fundamentals}

Reinforcement learning is the branch of machine learning where an agent learns by interacting with a simulated environment. At each time step the agent observes the environment, picks an action, receives a reward, and updates its policy so that the next action is more likely to lead to a higher long-run reward. The canonical text is \textcite{sutton2018reinforcement} (Development).

The formal frame is the Markov decision process (MDP), a five-tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of states, $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of actions, $\mathcal{P}(s' \mid s, a)$ is the transition kernel, $\mathcal{R}(s, a)$ is the reward, and $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ discounts future rewards. The two long-standing algorithm families are temporal-difference methods~\parencite{sutton1988td} and policy-gradient methods. \textcite{mnih2015dqn} popularised deep reinforcement learning by combining a deep neural network with $Q$-learning on Atari; \textcite{mnih2016asynchronous} introduced the asynchronous actor-critic A3C; \textcite{lillicrap2016continuous} introduced DDPG for continuous control; \textcite{haarnoja2018soft} introduced the off-policy Soft Actor-Critic. PPO~\parencite{schulman2017ppo} sits in the on-policy policy-gradient family.

\section{Policy-gradient methods and PPO}
\label{sec:ppo}

PPO is the standard policy-gradient algorithm at the time of writing (Development; the algorithm used by both the baseline and the probabilistic agent). Its core trick is to clip the policy update so one bad update cannot destroy the policy:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ppo_clip}
  \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \E_t \left[\min\left(\rho_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t, \; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_t\right) \right],
\end{equation}
where $\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)$, $\hat{A}_t$ is an estimate of the advantage at step $t$ and $\epsilon$ is a clipping parameter (typically $0.2$). PPO descends from Trust Region Policy Optimization~\parencite{schulman2015trpo} but replaces the constrained-optimisation step with a heuristic clip. PPO accommodates both discrete and continuous action spaces, which matters here because the action in the trading environment is a real number in $[-1, 1]$. The Stable-Baselines3 reference implementation~\parencite{raffin2021sb3} is used throughout (Development). None of the PPO variants in the recent literature are adopted; the contribution sits in the state-augmentation and constraint-enforcement coupling of Equation~\ref{eq:probabilistic_trade_size}, not in the optimiser.

\section{Safe, constrained and risk-sensitive reinforcement learning}
\label{sec:safe_rl}

The dissertation's contribution sits inside, and is positioned by, a substantial literature on reinforcement learning under safety, constraint, or risk-sensitive objectives. This section reviews the four sub-families and is the most important Positioning section in the chapter --- without this scaffolding the contribution of Equation~\ref{eq:probabilistic_trade_size} looks like an isolated heuristic, whereas it is best understood as a lightweight, model-free instance of the broader safe-RL programme.

\paragraph{Constrained MDPs.} \textcite{altman1999constrained} formalised the constrained MDP as an MDP with one or more auxiliary cost signals and a constraint on their long-run expectation (Positioning; the formal frame this dissertation's Equation~\ref{eq:constrained_objective} implicitly sits inside). \textcite{achiam2017cpo} introduced Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO), which extends TRPO with a quadratic constraint per update so the policy remains feasible after every update. \textcite{tessler2019reward} introduced Reward Constrained Policy Optimization, which converts a hard constraint into a learnable Lagrange multiplier added to the reward. \textcite{ray2019benchmarking} provide a benchmark suite for constrained RL. The dissertation's design rejects the Lagrangian route on credit-assignment grounds (Section~\ref{sec:soft_enforcement}) and uses an environment-side modification of the trade size instead --- a simpler, model-free enforcement that does not require gradients through the constraint.

\paragraph{Risk-sensitive RL.} \textcite{chow2015risk} optimise an objective penalised by the Conditional Value-at-Risk of the return distribution (Positioning; a competing way to bake risk into a reinforcement-learning policy, namely through reward shaping rather than environment-side scaling). \textcite{bellemare2017distributional} reframe value-function estimation as estimation of the full return distribution rather than just its expectation; \textcite{dabney2018qrdqn} approximate that distribution with quantile regression; \textcite{dabney2018iqn} extend it to implicit quantile networks. The dissertation is not a distributional-RL paper; the policy still optimises an expected return through PPO, and the uncertainty signal enters as a state feature and as a hard guard rather than as a quantile estimate. \textcite{garcia2015safe} survey the broader safe-RL landscape (Positioning).

\paragraph{Uncertainty-aware control.} \textcite{kahn2017uncertainty} use a Bayesian neural-network policy to avoid collisions in autonomous navigation; \textcite{lutjens2019safe} use model-uncertainty estimates to drive safer exploration; \textcite{depeweg2018decomposition} decompose Bayesian-deep-learning uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components to make risk-sensitive learning explicit. The dissertation borrows the aleatoric--epistemic decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty_estimation}) but uses it on the forecaster rather than on the policy itself, which keeps the contribution model-free with respect to the RL algorithm.

\paragraph{What none of the safe-RL literature solves directly for finance.} Constrained MDPs and risk-sensitive RL provide formal scaffolding but assume a known cost or risk signal; the contribution this dissertation makes is to derive that signal from a separate, probabilistic forecaster trained on the same data, rather than to encode it as a known cost. The closest prior work in the deep-learning-for-finance literature is reviewed next.

\section{Deep reinforcement learning for portfolio management}
\label{sec:drl_for_finance}

\textcite{moody2001learning} are foundational: they trained recurrent networks for portfolio choice via direct policy reinforcement, predating the deep-learning era by a decade (Positioning; the original ``direct RL for trading'' idea this dissertation extends with an uncertainty channel). \textcite{deng2017deep} extended this to deep direct reinforcement learning for trading on futures contracts. \textcite{jiang2017deep} applied policy-gradient methods to cryptocurrency portfolio selection with a price-tensor end-to-end policy (the asset class was chosen because rule-based baselines are weakest in crypto). \textcite{yang2020deep} extended the framework to US equities with an algorithm-level ensemble across A2C, PPO and DDPG; \textcite{liu2021finrl} contributed the FinRL open-source library; \textcite{liu2022finrlmeta} extended it to a benchmark suite with multiple market environments. \textcite{theate2021application} report a PPO-style trading agent on individual equities; \textcite{zhang2020deep} report a deep RL trader on futures markets; \textcite{daberius2019deep} report value-based and policy-based reinforcement learning for execution. \textcite{pricope2021drl} survey the literature; \textcite{sun2023reinforcement} and \textcite{hambly2023recent} survey it again with several years of hindsight, and both surveys flag uncertainty quantification as an open research direction. Risk-aware reward shaping is the most common existing answer (a Sharpe-ratio reward, a Sortino reward, a Conditional Value-at-Risk reward); explicit \emph{forecast-derived} uncertainty input as a state feature plus a hard guard is conspicuously absent from the surveys.

\section{Probabilistic time-series forecasting}
\label{sec:probabilistic_forecasting}

The probabilistic forecaster used in this dissertation is a stripped-down DeepAR~\parencite{salinas2020deepar} with a Gaussian head (Development). Three lineages converge on it. The first is the auto-regressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) family~\parencite{engle1982arch,bollerslev1986garch}, which models the time-varying variance of returns explicitly --- the foundational econometric statement that financial-return volatility is itself a forecastable time series. The second is the stylised-facts literature~\parencite{cont2001empirical} (Positioning), which catalogues the heavy tails, volatility clustering and slow long-range dependence that any forecaster of equity returns must contend with --- and which is exactly the reason the Gaussian assumption inside DeepAR has known limitations on assets with extreme kurtosis (Section~\ref{sec:phase2_full_grid} reports six RYTM failures arising from this). The third is the deep-state-space and probabilistic-deep-forecasting literature, which extends recurrent networks~\parencite{hochreiter1997lstm} to emit distributions rather than point estimates: DeepAR~\parencite{salinas2020deepar}, deep state-space models~\parencite{rangapuram2018deep}, N-BEATS~\parencite{oreshkin2020nbeats}, and the temporal fusion transformer~\parencite{lim2021temporal}.

\paragraph{What this dissertation uses.} The forecaster is a two-layer LSTM with hidden dimension 32 and two linear heads emitting predictive mean and predictive log-variance, trained by Gaussian negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gaussian_nll}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NLL}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \frac{(y_i - \mu_i)^2}{2 \sigma_i^2} + \frac{1}{2} \ln \sigma_i^2 \right].
\end{equation}
Its purpose is not to produce the most accurate possible forecast --- it is to supply a one-dimensional, model-based uncertainty signal that the PPO agent can condition on. The choice of the simplest probabilistic forecaster in the family is deliberate: it isolates the contribution of Equation~\ref{eq:probabilistic_trade_size} from the choice of forecaster architecture.

\section{Uncertainty estimation in deep learning}
\label{sec:uncertainty_estimation}

\paragraph{The aleatoric--epistemic decomposition.} \textcite{kendall2017uncertainties} formalise the distinction between two kinds of uncertainty in machine-learning predictions. \emph{Aleatoric} uncertainty arises from inherent noise in the data --- ``the market is choppy today, the next return could go either way''. \emph{Epistemic} uncertainty arises from the model itself --- ``today looks unfamiliar to my training data; I have not seen patterns like this before''. \textcite{depeweg2018decomposition} extend the decomposition to reinforcement-learning policies. The two are conceptually orthogonal and can be measured independently.

\paragraph{Likelihood-based methods (aleatoric).} The DeepAR approach of Section~\ref{sec:probabilistic_forecasting} captures aleatoric uncertainty directly: the predictive variance is part of the network's native output. This is what the Phase-1 headline experiments of this dissertation use.

\paragraph{Monte-Carlo dropout (epistemic).} \textcite{gal2016dropout} treat dropout at inference time as a variational approximation to a Bayesian posterior over the network weights. Sampling $K$ stochastic forward passes through the network gives an empirical distribution over predictions whose spread approximates the model's epistemic uncertainty. The advantage is one trained network rather than $K$; the disadvantage is a less rich posterior approximation than full variational inference.

\paragraph{Bayesian deep learning.} \textcite{blundell2015weight} introduced Bayes by Backprop, a variational-inference approach that learns a posterior over the weights directly. \textcite{maddox2019simple} introduce SWAG, a stochastic-weight-averaging approximation that produces a Gaussian over the weights with marginal cost. \textcite{ovadia2019can} benchmark several uncertainty estimators under distribution shift and find that none is uniformly best (Positioning).

\paragraph{Deep ensembles.} \textcite{lakshminarayanan2017simple} train several independent networks with different random initialisations and use their disagreement on a held-out point as the uncertainty proxy. The method is implementation-agnostic and captures both data-side and model-side uncertainty. The cost is $K$ times the training compute.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction.} \textcite{vovk2005algorithmic} take a distribution-free, calibration-set-based route to uncertainty: a small held-out calibration set maps any model's outputs into prediction intervals with marginal coverage guarantees. Conformal methods sit outside the parametric-likelihood family and are mentioned here as the third reasonable route this dissertation does not pursue.

\paragraph{Calibration.} \textcite{guo2017calibration} show that modern deep networks are systematically over-confident, with predicted softmax probabilities far from the empirical accuracy rates. The same calibration problem applies to predictive variance estimates: a Gaussian head with predictive standard deviation $\hat\sigma$ is calibrated only if the realised return falls inside $[\hat\mu - 1.96\hat\sigma, \hat\mu + 1.96\hat\sigma]$ on roughly 95\,\% of held-out days. Section~\ref{sec:forecast_uncertainty} reports the empirical calibration on the validation window.

\paragraph{What this dissertation uses.} The headline experiments use the aleatoric route (Equation~\ref{eq:gaussian_nll}). A third experimental arm, added in response to project-proposal feedback, adds Monte-Carlo dropout~\parencite{gal2016dropout} to the same LSTM to give an epistemic-uncertainty estimate combined with the aleatoric one per the Kendall--Gal decomposition. Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty_score} sets out the mathematical detail of both modes, and Section~\ref{sec:phase2_what_headline_shows} reports the empirical comparison.

\section{Backtest methodology and statistical robustness}
\label{sec:backtest_methodology}

A finance-RL contribution cannot be evaluated on a single test window without engaging the methodology literature that argues exactly such an evaluation is fragile. \textcite{bailey2014pseudo} document how easy it is to produce a back-tested strategy that looks excellent on a single window but has zero out-of-sample edge once multiple testing is accounted for. \textcite{bailey2014deflated} introduce the deflated Sharpe ratio, which discounts a realised Sharpe by the number of trials run and by the skew and kurtosis of the empirical return distribution. \textcite{harvey2016cross} make the same point about the published cross-section of expected returns: the field's cumulative multiple-testing burden suggests a conventional $t$-statistic threshold of three (not two) is the right hurdle for a new factor. \textcite{diebold1995comparing} provide a statistical test for the equality of predictive accuracy of two forecasters (the Diebold-Mariano test). \textcite{politis1994stationary} provide the stationary bootstrap that makes paired-resampling inference legitimate on serially correlated returns.

\paragraph{What this dissertation uses.} The evaluation protocol does three things to address the backtest-methodology critique. First, it pre-registers the protocol (data splits, seeds, ticker universe, metric set) in the configuration file \texttt{experiments/configs/dissertation\_protocol.json}; nothing in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is tuned on the test window. Second, it reports paired Wilcoxon and paired-bootstrap statistics on the matched-ticker sample (Section~\ref{sec:phase2_inference}), so the headline gap against the baseline carries an explicit $p$-value and a 95\,\% confidence interval rather than just a median. Third, it reports four out-of-time walk-forward folds (Section~\ref{sec:walk_forward}), so the test-window pick of 2022--2025 is not load-bearing. The dissertation does not report a deflated Sharpe ratio because the count of trials is small (three RL arms plus two rule comparators plus passive), but the limitation is named in Section~\ref{sec:threats_to_validity}.

\section{Gap and positioning}
\label{sec:gap_and_positioning}

The gap this dissertation closes is the absence, in prior deep-reinforcement-learning-for-finance work (the third column of Table~\ref{tab:lit_comparison}), of an explicit, model-based predictive-uncertainty signal that does two things at once: it enters the policy state, and it acts as a hard guard on new long-side actions. The contribution is positioned inside the safe-RL programme (Section~\ref{sec:safe_rl}) as a lightweight, model-free instance, and inside the probabilistic-forecasting programme (Section~\ref{sec:probabilistic_forecasting}) as a deliberately simple consumer of a calibrated forecaster's variance head. Section~\ref{sec:env_and_contribution} sets out the mathematical difference from a standard PPO baseline.

\paragraph{What this dissertation does not claim.} It does not claim a new reinforcement-learning algorithm; PPO is unchanged. It does not claim the probabilistic forecaster is novel; DeepAR is a 2020 idea and the LSTM is a 1997 idea. It does not claim multi-asset portfolio optimisation; the trading environment is single-asset and the multi-ticker robustness study runs one agent per ticker. It does not claim live-trading performance; all evidence is backtest evidence on adjusted-close daily data. The contribution is a controlled empirical study of one design choice on a reproducible protocol with named comparators and explicit paired-statistical inference.
