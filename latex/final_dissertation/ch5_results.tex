\chapter{Empirical Results \& Analysis}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports every experiment in the order the methodology was
built: algorithm validation on games with known outcomes
(Section~\ref{sec:res_phase0}); a pilot study with a deliberately minimal
state whose failure motivates the final design
(Section~\ref{sec:res_pilot}); and the main comparison of from-scratch
REINFORCE and Deep Q-learning on the complete 9-feature state
(Section~\ref{sec:res_main}), followed by the fee-sensitivity study
(Section~\ref{sec:res_fees}), a preliminary risk-aware-reward experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:res_riskaware}) and the consolidated analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:res_analysis}). All numbers are produced by committed
scripts from cached data; none are illustrative placeholders. Complete
per-month results for every method appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:permonth}.

For a reader keeping only one question in mind, let it be this one:
\emph{did any learned agent do better than simply buying at the start of
the month and holding to the end?} Everything in this chapter is
organised to answer that question fairly --- and the honest answer turns
out to be more interesting than a yes or a no.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:res_setup}

\paragraph{Data and split.} SPY daily closes 2018--2024; one calendar
month of daily bars per episode; chronological split of 58 training / 26
held-out test months (test $=$ November 2022 -- December 2024).

\paragraph{Protocol.} Initial cash $C_0 = \$10{,}000$; proportional fee
$c = 5$ bps; trade slice $\Delta = 0.1\,C_0$; training budget 80{,}000
environment steps per method per seed; seeds $\{42, 43, 44\}$; greedy
masked evaluation. Metrics and baselines as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\section{Phase 0: algorithm validation on games}
\label{sec:res_phase0}

Before any market experiment, the learning implementations were validated
on tasks with unambiguous success criteria
(Table~\ref{tab:res_phase0}). Every learner clears its random floor. Two
observations carry forward. First, from-scratch REINFORCE learns but
plateaus below the more stabilised methods on CartPole (292 vs.\ 500) ---
the textbook variance limitation of Monte-Carlo policy gradients,
visible in our own runs. Second, tabular Q-learning solves CartPole
\emph{only after} hand-discretizing the 4-dimensional continuous state;
the 9-dimensional continuous trading state makes any such table
infeasible, which is precisely why the value-based track uses a deep
Q-network (Section~\ref{sec:dqn}).

\section{Pilot study: the minimal state collapses to buy-and-hold}
\label{sec:res_pilot}

The pilot used the minimal 3-feature state
$s = [\Delta P, C, n]$ with whole-share actions and the same wealth-change
reward, split, fee and budget. Three policy-gradient optimisers ---
from-scratch REINFORCE, A2C and PPO --- were trained on the identical MDP.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Pilot (minimal 3-D state, whole shares): all learned policies
execute an action sequence identical to fully-invested buy-and-hold on
every one of the 26 test months.}
\label{tab:res_pilot}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Mean $\Delta W$ (\$)} & \textbf{Trades/mo} & \textbf{Months identical to buy-max} \\
\midrule
Buy-max-hold (baseline) & 76.91 & 19.1 & 26/26 (by definition) \\
REINFORCE (scratch)     & 76.91 & 19.1 & 26/26 \\
A2C                     & 76.91 & 19.1 & 26/26 \\
PPO                     & 76.91 & 19.1 & 26/26 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two lessons shaped the final design. \emph{Baseline design is itself a
result}: against a naive one-share buy-and-hold baseline (mean
$\Delta W = \$7.97$), any of these agents would have appeared to win
$10\times$; only the fair fully-invested baseline reveals that they had
simply rediscovered buy-and-hold. \emph{The collapse is a property of the
objective and the state, not of any single optimiser}: three different
policy-gradient methods found the same optimum. The final model therefore
enriches the state (Section~\ref{sec:state}) and adds a value-based
learner as a structurally different second method.

\paragraph{REINFORCE rediscovers buy-and-hold --- exactly.}
The REINFORCE policy's executed trades match fully-invested buy-and-hold
to the cent on all 26 test months (win rate vs.\ B1b $= 0$ because the
wealth paths are identical; Figure~\ref{fig:res_paths}, dashed line).
Even with entry, clock, trend and risk features available, the
Monte-Carlo policy gradient converges to ``invest everything immediately
and hold.'' This is not an implementation failure; it is the risk-neutral
optimum. Under $r_t = \Delta W_t$, expected return is maximised by
maximum exposure whenever expected drift is positive --- and the
2018--2022 training window drifts upward on average. The 9-feature state
changes what the policy \emph{could} express, but not what the objective
\emph{rewards}.

\paragraph{Deep Q deviates --- modestly, and in the direction of risk
control.} The DQN policy executes a different action sequence in every
month, wins against buy-and-hold in 9/26 months (predominantly falling
ones), and achieves lower monthly standard deviation (274 vs.\ 310),
lower maximum drawdown (4.8\% vs.\ 6.0\%) and higher monthly Sharpe
(0.45 vs.\ 0.40), at a cost of \$1.96/month of mean return.
Figure~\ref{fig:res_exposure} shows the mechanism on the worst test
month: both agents ramp to near-full investment, but as the decline
develops Deep Q sells down to $\approx 40\%$ exposure while buy-and-hold
stays fully invested. Mechanistically, bootstrapped one-step targets make
DQN sensitive to short-horizon state distinctions (local volatility,
negative momentum) that the Monte-Carlo return signal averages away; the
learned behaviour is ``fully invested by default, trim around
turbulence.''

\paragraph{PPO is bimodal across seeds.} Because a two-mode claim needs
more than three samples, PPO was run on five seeds. On seeds 43, 44 and
45 it converged to \emph{exact} buy-and-hold (26/26 identical months,
$\Delta W = \$125.19$); on seeds 42 and 46 it converged to a cautious
low-exposure policy ($\Delta W \approx \$56$, $\approx 4$ trades/month,
MDD $\approx 1.9\%$) --- each mode replicated at least twice, with
nothing in between. Both corners are rational under the risk-neutral
objective; which one PPO reaches depends on its early rollouts, because
ratio clipping deliberately limits how far each update can move the
policy --- slow movement stabilises training but can freeze the policy
near wherever the early data pointed. This seed sensitivity is itself an
ablation finding: the two scratch methods were directionally consistent
across all seeds; the clipped method was not.

Across seeds, REINFORCE sits at or within a few dollars of the
buy-and-hold corner every time (its seed-43/44 variants differ only by a

more trading and equal-or-better risk metrics; PPO oscillates between
the two rational corners.

\paragraph{Training dynamics.} Figure~\ref{fig:res_curves} shows episode
returns during training for the scratch methods. Unlike the Phase-0
games, the curves do not ``rise and saturate'': each training episode is
a randomly drawn month whose achievable return is dominated by that
month's drift, so the reward signal is noisy by construction. This is the
practical face of the high-variance problem analysed in
Section~\ref{sec:pg_derivation}, and a key difference between trading and
game benchmarks: the environment's randomness overwhelms the visible
learning signal, and evaluation on held-out data --- not the training
curve --- is the only trustworthy progress measure.

\section{Fee sensitivity}
\label{sec:res_fees}

To test whether the learned behaviours respond to economic friction,
REINFORCE and Deep Q were retrained (seed 42) at three fee levels and
evaluated at the same fee (Table~\ref{tab:res_fees},
Figure~\ref{fig:res_fees}).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering

  \caption{Mean $\Delta W$ under increasing proportional fees.}
  \label{fig:res_fees}

Two observations. First, REINFORCE tracks buy-and-hold identically at
every fee level --- consistent with its collapse: a policy that trades
exactly like buy-and-hold pays exactly buy-and-hold's fees. Second,
Deep Q \emph{adapts its trading intensity to the fee}: 19.8 trades/month
when trading is free, 13.2 at 5 bps, and only 6.1 at 20 bps. The
direction of the adaptation is economically correct (the fee enters the
reward, and the agent learns to economise on it), although at 20 bps the
residual trading still costs more than it saves relative to buy-and-hold
--- risk trimming is not free, and a risk-neutral objective gives the
agent no credit for the variance it removes.

\section{Preliminary evidence: a risk-aware reward}
\label{sec:res_riskaware}

The analysis so far repeatedly traces behaviour back to the risk-neutral
reward. As a first, deliberately exploratory test of that attribution,
both scratch methods were retrained (seed 42, all else identical) on a
drawdown-penalised reward,
\[
r'_t \;=\; \Delta W_t \;-\; \lambda \,\max\!\bigl(0,\; D_t - D_{t-1}\bigr),
\qquad D_t = \max_{u \le t} W_u - W_t,\quad \lambda = 0.25,
\]
which charges the agent a quarter of every new dollar of drawdown from
its peak wealth. Crucially, evaluation still uses the \emph{true} wealth
metrics, so Table~\ref{tab:res_riskaware} is directly comparable to
Table~\ref{tab:res_main}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Risk-aware preliminary (seed 42, $\lambda = 0.25$; trained on
the shaped reward, evaluated on true wealth metrics).}
\label{tab:res_riskaware}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Mean $\Delta W$} & \textbf{Std}
 & \textbf{Trades/mo} & \textbf{Sharpe} & \textbf{MDD} \\
\midrule
B1b buy-and-hold (reference) & 125.19 & 309.87 & 11.0 & 0.40 & 6.0\% \\
REINFORCE (risk-aware)       & 0.00   & 0.00   & 0.0  & ---  & 0.0\% \\
Deep Q (risk-aware)          & 49.48  & 73.22  & 10.1 & \textbf{0.68} & \textbf{1.4\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The outcome sharpens the dissertation's central claim in an unexpected
way. REINFORCE does not become a risk manager --- it \emph{jumps to the
opposite corner}, converging to total abstention (zero trades, zero
wealth change): under its episodic Monte-Carlo signal, once drawdowns are
taxed at $\lambda = 0.25$ the all-cash policy dominates, and the
corner-seeking character of the optimiser is unchanged --- only the
corner moves. Deep Q, by contrast, delivers precisely the trade the
shaped reward requests: maximum drawdown collapses from 6.0\% to 1.4\%
and the monthly Sharpe ratio rises from 0.40 to 0.68, in exchange for
roughly 60\% of the mean return. One seed and one $\lambda$ make this
preliminary evidence, not a study --- but the direction confirms both
the reward-attribution argument and the family-level behavioural
difference, and defines the obvious next experiment (a $\lambda$ sweep,
Chapter~6).

\section{Analysis and key findings}
\label{sec:res_analysis}

\paragraph{1. The reward, not the state (or the algorithm), sets the
incentive.} The pilot and the final model bracket this cleanly. With a
minimal state, every optimiser collapsed to buy-and-hold. With the
complete state, the Monte-Carlo policy gradient \emph{still} chose
buy-and-hold --- because under a risk-neutral wealth-change reward in a
rising regime, buy-and-hold is (near-)optimal, and REINFORCE optimises
exactly that objective. Beating buy-and-hold on raw return in this setup
would be evidence of a bug or of leakage, not of skill.

\paragraph{2. Algorithm families respond differently to the same
objective.} Under a matched budget, data split, fee and seed: REINFORCE's
episodic Monte-Carlo signal drives it to the exposure corner; Deep Q's
per-step bootstrapping yields a policy that reacts to local state, buys
measurable risk reduction at negligible return cost, and adapts its
trading intensity to transaction fees; PPO's clipped updates make its
destination seed-dependent, landing on either rational corner. This
family-level behavioural characterisation is the central empirical
finding of the dissertation.

\paragraph{3. Honest baselines remain the load-bearing methodology.}
Every headline in this chapter depends on the fully-invested baseline
being present. The same table with only a one-share or do-nothing
baseline would report a spurious $10\times$ victory. Equally, the Sharpe
column shows how a naive risk-adjusted comparison can mislead: the
random and PPO (seed 42) rows post the highest Sharpe ratios simply
because low exposure shrinks variance faster than it shrinks mean.

\paragraph{4. What a risk-aware reward would change.} Deep Q's behaviour
--- exposure trimming that reduces drawdown at small return cost --- is
exactly the behaviour a reward of the form
$r_t = \Delta W_t - \lambda D_t$ (drawdown-penalised) would
\emph{explicitly} pay for. Under the current risk-neutral reward, that
behaviour survives only where the value function finds it locally
profitable; under a risk-aware reward it would be globally incentivised.
This is the single highest-leverage extension (Chapter~6).

\paragraph{Scope.} Single asset; daily bars in monthly episodes; three
seeds; a risk-neutral reward; PPO from a reference library rather than
from scratch. Consequences and remedies are discussed in Chapter~6.

\paragraph{Chapter summary (plain language).} Both home-built learners
passed their video-game driving test, so their behaviour on markets can
be trusted to reflect the problem, not bugs. On real market data the
policy-gradient learner concluded that the best plan was to buy
immediately and hold --- exactly matching the passive strategy, trade for
trade --- and the mathematics says that conclusion is \emph{correct} for
the reward it was given, since on average the market drifted upward. The
value-based learner found something subtler: it stayed almost fully
invested but sold down during turbulent stretches, losing about \$2 a
month of profit in exchange for noticeably smaller worst-case losses ---
and when trading fees were raised, it traded less, all of its own accord.
The modern PPO method landed on one of two sensible answers depending on
its random starting conditions. Finally, a first experiment with a
reward that punishes losses directly showed the value-based learner
cutting its worst losses four-fold --- while the policy-gradient learner
simply switched from ``always fully invested'' to ``never invest at
all.'' None of this ``beats the market'' --- and the study was designed
so that saying so honestly is its central strength. What these findings
mean, and what they do not, is the subject of the final chapter.
