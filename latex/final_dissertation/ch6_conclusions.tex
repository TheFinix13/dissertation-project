\chapter{Conclusions \& Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{Achievements against objectives}
\label{sec:conc_objectives}

All five objectives of Section~\ref{sec:intro_objectives} were met, though
O4 and O5 were met with a negative answer, which is discussed rather than
glossed over below.

\textbf{O1}: the trading MDP was formulated with a nine-feature state that
is complete with respect to the defined environment, a divisible-asset
\texttt{Discrete(3)} action model with feasibility masking, and a
fee-inclusive wealth-change reward. The reward's telescoping property
($\sum_t \Delta W_t = W_T - W_0$) is verified numerically in every episode.
Each feature is justified against stated admissibility criteria, and the
environment refuses to instantiate a state-reward pairing that violates
them.

\textbf{O2}: both learning rules were derived from first principles,
including the likelihood-ratio derivation explaining the $\log\pi_\theta$
term, and implemented from scratch in PyTorch with no reinforcement-learning
library in the training path.

\textbf{O3}: both implementations were validated on CartPole, Flappy Bird
and LunarLander, clearing their random floors on all three.

\textbf{O4}: all three methods were evaluated on 24 held-out months of SPY
data against four reference strategies, under a three-way chronological
split, matched 80{,}000-step budgets and six seeds. The action model was
calibrated and the state ladder settled on a validation year that the test
months never informed.

\textbf{O5}: the observed behaviour was analysed mechanistically. The
analysis is the part of this project that changed most in the course of
doing it. The first credible-looking results turned out to rest on
twelve implementation defects, and correcting them replaced an apparent
risk-adjusted victory with a measurable shortfall.

\section{Principal findings}
\label{sec:conc_findings}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The reward, not the state, is the binding constraint.}
  Under $r_t = \Delta W_t$ on an asset with positive drift, the optimal
  policy is unconditional maximum exposure, so no state can improve on the
  smallest and additional features add only parameters and variance. This
  was predicted in Section~\ref{sec:reward} before any run and is supported
  by three independent results: the ladder does not separate rungs from one
  feature to twelve; adding drawdown to the state without adding it to the
  reward changes nothing beyond seed noise; and the direct policy optimisers
  converge on policies that ignore the state entirely. This is the empirical
  face of an old theoretical point \cite{moody2001}: the reward defines the
  incentive, and an agent can only be faulted for optimising what it was
  asked to optimise.

  \item \textbf{Algorithm family determines whether the state is used at
  all.} REINFORCE is mask-determined in every seed and PPO in almost every
  seed, on validation and test alike. Both converge on rules of the form
  ``buy whenever legal'' or ``never trade'', reading only which actions are
  feasible. Deep Q-learning conditions on its state in every run of every
  cell. That the collapse afflicts a modern clipped method as well as the
  textbook one rules out gradient variance as the explanation. What the two
  share is that both optimise a policy directly against an objective whose
  best unconditional answer is already close to optimal.
  The methodological consequence is general: a comparison between state
  representations is meaningless for a method that ignores its state. And
  because a state-blind policy scores well in a rising market, returns alone
  cannot reveal this.

  \item \textbf{Lower drawdown through lower exposure is not risk
  management.} Deep Q-learning attains 41\% of buy-and-hold's return while
  incurring 40\% of its drawdown, so the ratio is essentially unchanged and
  its Sharpe ratio is lower, 0.507 against 0.638. Adding a drawdown penalty
  to the reward moves both figures down together and lowers Sharpe further.
  The penalty works exactly as its algebra requires and not as intended,
  because abstention is the cheapest way to comply with a reward that
  penalises loss without crediting return per unit of risk. Reporting mean
  exposure beside every return is what makes this checkable; a table showing
  a threefold drawdown reduction without it would read as an achievement.

  \item \textbf{Evaluation design is a result in itself, and this project
  demonstrates it against its own earlier work.} An audit found twelve
  defects, and the two largest both flattered the results. A benchmark
  labelled buy-and-hold deployed capital a tenth at a time, understating
  itself by 21.6\%. An action granularity capped attainable exposure at
  0.69 against the benchmark's 0.91, making the benchmark unbeatable in
  principle regardless of skill. Correcting them
  converted an apparent risk-adjusted victory into a measurable shortfall.
  This is the failure mode the financial machine-learning methodology
  literature warns of \cite{bailey2014, lopezdeprado2018}, encountered from
  the inside rather than cited.

  \item \textbf{Variance is a finding, not a nuisance.} Learned outcomes are
  bimodal across seeds rather than scattered about a mean. That is why the
  seed count was raised to six, why ranges are reported rather than standard
  errors, and why the ladder's stopping rule terminates immediately. The
  stopping rule compares differences against seed spread, and the spread is
  larger than any difference between representations. A single-seed study of
  this design could have supported almost any conclusion.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Critical appraisal and limitations}
\label{sec:conc_appraisal}

\paragraph{What is limited.} (i) A single asset and a single market-regime
window. Conclusions drawn from 2024--2025, a period that rose overall, need
not transfer to other assets or eras. In particular, the finding that
state-blind maximum exposure is hard to beat is partly a property of that
drift. (ii) Daily bars in monthly episodes rather than the intraday
$T{=}390$ horizon originally envisaged; the environment interface is
horizon-agnostic, but the empirical claims hold only at the tested
granularity. (iii) Six seeds characterise the bimodality but do not
estimate the probability of each mode with any precision; a study whose
outcomes are bimodal really wants tens of runs. (iv) Episodes are treated as
independent although consecutive months are correlated; the MDP framework
used here has no mechanism for that temporal structure. (v) The state,
however deliberately designed, remains a partial observation of the market:
the problem is strictly a POMDP \cite{kaelbling1998}, and no belief-state or
recurrent machinery was used. (vi) Execution is idealised, with fills at the
close and unlimited liquidity at the quoted price; for SPY at these trade
sizes the idealisation is mild, but it is an idealisation. (vii) The
risk-aware reward is represented by a single functional form at a single
selected weight, so the negative result concerns that penalty and not
risk-sensitive reinforcement learning in general.

\paragraph{What could have been done differently.} Three choices would
change with hindsight, and the first is the substantial one.

The action model should have been calibrated against the episode length
before any agent was trained. The exposure ceiling
(Section~\ref{sec:res_calibration}) is arithmetic, computable in minutes,
and requires no learning and no test data. Had it been computed at the
outset, an entire round of experiments in which the benchmark was
unreachable would not have been run. The conclusion drawn from that
round would not have needed retracting.

Second, the verification effort was directed at the wrong layer for too
long. The environment's unit tests passed throughout, because they tested
that the accounting was internally consistent. The defects that mattered
were not accounting errors. They were mismatches between what a component
guaranteed and what the experiment assumed it guaranteed: features computed
per-episode rather than continuously, a baseline whose name did not describe
its behaviour, a reward depending on a quantity absent from the state. Tests
written against stated properties rather than against code paths would have
caught all three, which is how the suite is now organised.

Third, the risk-neutral reward should have been paired with a risk-aware
variant as a designed experiment from the start, and paired with the
admissibility analysis that shows which combinations are even well-posed.
The right question, whether the state can matter when the objective does
not reward conditioning, arrived late in the project. It is therefore
answered at one point on the frontier rather than across it.

\paragraph{What the project got right, in hindsight.} Validating the
implementations on games before markets meant that when the agents failed to
beat the market, that failure could be attributed to the problem rather than
to the code. Reporting two passive references separately, rather than one
labelled buy-and-hold, is what exposed REINFORCE's near-benchmark return as
the slice schedule instead of a near-miss at market timing. Reporting mean
exposure beside every return is what distinguished de-risking from skill.
Deriving the losses before implementing them was, as the supervision process
insisted, the difference between using an algorithm and understanding one.
And auditing the project's own results rather than defending them is what
makes the numbers in Chapter~5 worth reading.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:conc_future}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Risk-aware rewards.} Replacing $r_t = \Delta W_t$ with a
        drawdown-penalised variant or the differential Sharpe ratio
        \cite{moody2001} changes the incentive that currently makes
        buy-and-hold optimal. Section~\ref{sec:res_risk} reports one point
        on that frontier and shows the shape of the difficulty: the
        penalty weight controls a trade-off that collapses quickly, with
        abstention rather than better timing as the agent's easiest reply.
        The natural continuation is a reward that penalises variability
        without rewarding inactivity, such as the differential Sharpe ratio
        \cite{moody2001}, evaluated across the frontier rather than at a
        single weight.
  \item \textbf{Intraday horizons.} Minute-bar episodes ($T{=}390$) via
        the same environment API, testing whether the family-level
        behavioural differences persist at higher frequency, where
        fee pressure and state dynamics are sharper.
  \item \textbf{Partial observability.} Recurrent policies, or an
        explicit regime model in the spirit of hidden Markov models
        \cite{rabiner1989}, to address the POMDP structure acknowledged
        throughout.
  \item \textbf{Continuous actions and portfolios.} Replacing the fixed
        trade slice with a continuous allocation, and extending to
        multi-asset weight vectors with risk-adjusted objectives
        \cite{markowitz1952, jiang2017}.
  \item \textbf{Value-based refinements.} Double Q-learning
        \cite{vanhasselt2016} to control the overestimation bias that
        masked TD targets partially mitigate here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reflection on project management}
\label{sec:conc_pm}

The project followed a staged plan agreed in supervision, summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:conc_plan}. Two properties of the plan proved decisive.
First, \emph{stage gates with demonstrable artefacts}: no stage began
until the previous one produced something checkable (passing tests,
learning curves, result tables), which localised every fault to the stage
that introduced it. Second, \emph{descoping as a deliberate act}: the
most consequential replanning decision was narrowing an early
multi-component ambition (uncertainty-aware portfolio management with a
forecasting subsystem) to a single, fully understood trading MDP with two
from-scratch algorithms. The decision was made with the supervisor,
explicitly trading breadth of feature count for depth of understanding.
The dissertation's examinable content vindicates that trade.

Risk management was handled by construction rather than by contingency
tables. The riskiest claim in any learning project, that the implementation
is correct, was attacked at two levels. Early, the agents were required to
solve games with known answers; late, the audit found twelve defects in
code whose own tests were passing. The riskiest scientific claim, that the
agent beats the market, was constrained by reference strategies designed to
make it hard to assert falsely. That is precisely why the claim was
withdrawn when the references were fixed.

The audit is worth naming as a project-management outcome rather than only a
technical one, because it cost roughly a week that was not in the plan and it
invalidated work already written up. The alternative, publishing the earlier
numbers, would have produced a more flattering dissertation that could not
survive its own viva. The exposure ceiling that made the benchmark
unreachable is a single line of arithmetic that any examiner could reproduce.
Weekly supervision meetings served as checkpoints throughout, and the final
suite was engineered to be regenerable in minutes on a laptop CPU, so that
every number in this dissertation can be recomputed live.

\paragraph{Closing summary (plain language).} Three learning programs were
built, taught to play video games until they could prove they worked, and
then set loose on eight years of real market prices with imaginary money.
They were given a deliberately rich view of their situation: how the price
had been moving, how choppy it had been, how much cash and stock they held,
how their current position was doing, and how much of the month was left.

None of them beat simply buying at the start of the month and holding. Two
of the three did something more interesting than losing: they stopped looking
at the information altogether and settled into a fixed habit, buying whenever
they were allowed to. That is not a malfunction. They were rewarded purely for
making money, the price generally went up, and under those conditions the best
possible habit is to be fully invested and never think about it. The third
program did keep watching its situation and did suffer smaller losses in bad
stretches, but only because it kept less money in the market, which anyone can
do without learning anything.

The most useful finding is therefore not about markets but about how such
experiments go wrong. An earlier version of this project appeared to succeed,
and the appearance came from two mistakes. The yardstick it was measured
against had been quietly handicapped, and the agents had been given a trading
mechanism too slow to match the yardstick even in principle. Both mistakes
flattered the result. Finding them, rather than publishing around them, is
what the rest of this dissertation is built on. In this field, the
experiments that report victory are usually the ones with the error still in
them.
