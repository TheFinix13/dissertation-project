\section{Introduction}

This chapter reports and analyses the experiment results from the
trading environment and learning algorithms described in Chapter 4. The
experiments examine the financial performance of the learned agents, the
effects of two state representations, their reward functions, and the
role of the training data itself.

The experiments are structured around five questions that directly
relate to the objectives of the dissertation to increase portfolio
wealth:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Performance:} Do the learned agents outperform appropriate
  simple benchmarks on unseen data after accounting for transaction
  costs?
\item
  \textbf{Behaviour:} Do the learned agents make use of the market and
  portfolio information provided in the state representations?
\item
  \textbf{State representation:} Does adding the drawdown feature to the
  main nine-feature state affect the learned behaviour or financial
  performance?
\item
  \textbf{Reward:} Does the risk-aware reward improve the balance
  between return and drawdown, rather than simply encouraging the agent
  to trade less?
\item
  \textbf{Data:} Do the size and balance of the training data explain
  the behaviour of the learned agents, and does fixing the data fix the
  behaviour?
\end{enumerate}

The chapter answers these questions with two studies.
The \textbf{first study} trained the agents on the 60 real training
months and evaluated them on the held-out 2024--2025 months. Its results
were negative, and Section 5.4 shows that the negative results share one
cause: 60 episodes cannot train networks of this size. The \textbf{main
study} corrects this. The agents are trained on 3,000 balanced simulated
episodes from the generator of Chapter 4, evaluated under controlled
market regimes, and finally transferred, unchanged, to 180 real months
that include the 2008 financial crisis. Every experimental question the
first study asked is asked again in the main study, under data that can
support an answer.

A key part of the analysis is determining whether the learned strategies
outperform simple strategies. Buy-and-hold, for example, is an important
benchmark here. A learned agent producing a positive return does not
necessarily indicate that it has learned a useful trading strategy if a
simple investment strategy achieves a similar or better result.

For this reason, the analysis considers both \textbf{financial
performance} and \textbf{agent behaviour}. Financial performance is
evaluated using measures including wealth change, exposure, trading
activity, drawdown, and Sharpe ratio. Behavioural analysis examines
whether an agent\textquotesingle s actions change as the state changes.
This helps to identify a policy that responds to state information
rather than one that follows a fixed trading pattern. The next section
sets out the protocols shared by both studies.

\section{Experiment Protocols and Evaluation Criteria}

This section describes how the experiments in this chapter were
conducted and how the reported results should be interpreted.

\subsection{Experimental Configurations}

Each configuration trained REINFORCE, Deep Q-learning (DQN), and
Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) separately in the portfolio
environment. The main study trains each seed for 300,000 steps on the
simulated episodes; the first study trained for 80,000 steps on the real
months, a budget sized to its far smaller dataset.

To account for randomness in reinforcement learning~\cite{henderson2018}, six random seeds
were used, with an initial capital of \(C_{0} = \$ 10,000\) at the start
of every monthly episode. This means six independent training runs all
with the same configuration, allowing us to analyse the consistency of
each algorithm's results rather than relying on a single training run.

During evaluation, all policies acted deterministically: policy-gradient
algorithms select the action with the highest probability, while
value-based algorithms select the action with the highest estimated
value.

Every experimental decision in this chapter was made on validation data.
In the first study, the 2023 months carried these decisions. In the main
study, the 300 simulated validation episodes carry them: the trade size
in Section 5.5, the risk-penalty weight in Section 5.8, and the
transaction-fee robustness check in Section 5.9. The simulated test
episodes and the real transfer months made no configuration decision and
are each evaluated once.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

There are five financial measures used in each experiment report. They
are averaged over the episodes and, for learned agents, across the six
training seeds:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Mean wealth change \((\Delta W)\) reported in dollars per episode;
\item
  Mean exposure \((\Delta v_{t})\), which represents the proportion of
  initial capital invested in the asset and is averaged across trading
  days;
\item
  Trades per episode;
\item
  Mean episode drawdown \((\Delta{dd}_{t})\);
\item
  and the monthly Sharpe ratio~\cite{sharpe1994}, a financial metric that calculates the
  average return relative to the variation of those daily returns during
  the month.
\end{itemize}

The tables also report the \textbf{seed spread}, which is the difference
between the best and worst result across the six seeds. This is included
because there might be large differences between training runs which the
average alone may hide~\cite{henderson2018}. A large spread indicates that the algorithm did
not produce a consistent trading behaviour across the different runs.

Financial performance is then followed by a behavioural analysis, as
returns alone cannot show whether a policy actually used the information
in its state. For example, a policy that buys simply because buying is
allowed can perform well during a rising market without responding to
any market information.

This led to \textbf{state-dependence analysis,} which was used to test
whether a policy\textquotesingle s actions changed when its observed
state changed while the valid actions remained the same. For instance,
if a policy selected different actions at different steps with the same
actions available, the difference could be attributed to the observed
state. The tables report how many of the six seeds met this condition.

\subsection{Benchmark Strategies}

Two non-learning strategies are used as benchmarks. They use the same
trading environment, portfolio conditions, and transaction fees as the
learned agents.

\textbf{Buy-and-hold} invests the full initial capital on the first
trading day and holds the position until the end of the month, when the
position is closed. This is the main benchmark as it is a simple passive
investment strategy.

\textbf{Always-buy} \(\mathbf{(0.25}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{0}}\mathbf{)}\)
purchases a fixed trade amount equal to 25\% of the initial capital
whenever buying is available, continuing until it has fully invested in
that asset. This strategy is useful for comparing against policies that
achieve their returns through repeated buying rather than by using
information from its observed state, as it also does not use any market
or portfolio information when deciding whether to buy.

With the protocols fixed, the next section reports the first study and
what it revealed.

\section{The First Study: Real Data Alone}

This section reports the first study: the three algorithms trained on
the 60 real training months and tested on the 24 held-out months of
2024--2025. The first study ran the full experimental programme --- trade-size
calibration, behavioural analysis, the state and reward comparisons, and
a fee sweep --- and this section reports what each part found. The
findings are presented compactly because Section 5.4 shows they share a
single cause, and the main study re-examines every one of them under
corrected data.

\subsection{Setup of the First Study}

The first study used the larger network of two 128-unit hidden layers and a
budget of 80,000 training steps per seed. Its trade size was calibrated
on the 2023 validation months by the exposure-ceiling rule described in
Section 5.5: a Buy commits a fixed fraction of initial capital, so a
small trade size caps the exposure an agent can reach within a month.
On the validation months, the \(0.25C_{0}\) size was the smallest that
reached at least 90\% of buy-and-hold's exposure and was fixed for all
experiments.

The remaining first-study experiments reinforced the same picture, and three
results summarise them. First, adding the drawdown feature to the state
changed mean wealth by less than the seed spread for every algorithm
(REINFORCE \(-\$4.12\), Deep Q +\$3.13, both against spreads above
\$24), so the ten-feature state offered no measurable advantage under
the wealth-change reward. Second, the risk-aware reward
(\(\lambda = 0.10\), selected on validation) cut Deep Q's drawdown by
41\% but only by cutting its exposure roughly in half; its Sharpe ratio
fell from 0.505 to 0.416, so the penalty bought less risk with less
participation, not better risk management. Third, the fee sweep from 0
to 50 basis points showed only Deep Q-learning reducing its trading in
response (10.9 to 1.9 trades per month, an 83\% reduction), consistent
with it being the only method whose policies read their state.

Even where its state-dependence should have paid, it did not. March 2025
was the worst test month: buy-and-hold lost \$398.41. Deep Q-learning
held a mean exposure of 0.490 that month, higher than its overall
average of 0.312, and lost \$184.04 --- about the same one-third scaling
of the benchmark it showed everywhere else. Its lower drawdown came from
holding less overall, not from recognising when to step aside.

The first study therefore ends with a puzzle. The only method that
demonstrably used its state earned the least, and the methods that
ignored their state earned the most. The next section resolves the
puzzle.

\section{The Data Bottleneck}

This section explains why the first study's results look the way they do. The
explanation is not about any single algorithm. It is about how little
data the agents were given.

The training split contains 60 monthly episodes. The first study's networks
have more than 18,000 trainable parameters. A model with that much
capacity can reproduce 60 training months exactly, the way a student who
memorises 60 practice problems scores perfectly on those problems and
then fails a fresh exam. Nothing forces the network to learn a rule that
transfers, because memorising the answers is cheaper.

The training months are also imbalanced. Twenty-nine of the 60 months
rose by more than 2\% and only 16 fell by more than 2\%, and the test
years 2024--2025 rise almost throughout. Under that distribution,
``always buy'' is a nearly optimal answer that requires no state
information at all. This is the same trap as training a classifier on a
dataset that is 99\% one class: predicting the majority class scores
well and learns nothing. The always-buy policies of the policy-gradient
agents in Section 5.3 are exactly this artefact.

The same reasoning explains why Deep Q-learning performed worst on the
real test months. A value-based method must estimate the value of each
state it visits, which needs many visits to similar states under a
stable state distribution. Sixty episodes from a shifting market provide
neither. The behavioural finding stands --- six of six DQN seeds did
condition on their state --- but the values behind those decisions were
estimated from too little data, so conditioning on them did not pay.

None of this can be tested on the real data, because the real data is
the problem. Testing it needs a setting where the number of episodes,
the balance of market conditions, and the match between training and
test distributions are all controlled. That is what the main study
provides: 3,000 balanced simulated episodes, a network shrunk from
18,000 parameters to 2,800, and controlled per-regime testing, with the
real months kept back as a final transfer test. The remainder of this
chapter reports it, repeating each of its experiments under the corrected
data.

\section{Fixed Trade Size Under the Simulated Data}

Before the algorithms are compared, the fixed transaction size is
re-calibrated on the simulated validation episodes, exactly as the first study
calibrated it on the 2023 months. The trade size matters because each
Buy commits a fixed fraction of initial capital, so the size sets a hard
ceiling on the exposure an agent can reach within a 21-bar episode. A
policy judged against buy-and-hold must first be able to reach
buy-and-hold's exposure in principle; otherwise a poor result reports
arithmetic, not learning.

The calibration needs no training. For each candidate size, a policy
that buys whenever buying is valid measures the highest exposure that
size permits. The selection rule is the first study's rule: the smallest size
whose ceiling reaches 90\% of buy-and-hold's exposure. Table 5.2 shows
the outcome on the 300 simulated validation episodes.

\section{Main Results: The Controlled Regime Comparison}

This section reports the central experiment of the dissertation: the
three algorithms trained on the 3,000 balanced simulated episodes and
evaluated on 600 fresh episodes, 200 in each market regime. Six seeds
per algorithm, 300,000 steps each, with the pyramid network of about
2,800 parameters.

\subsection{Benchmarks Under Balanced Regimes}

The balanced test set changes what the two benchmarks earn, and this
change is the point of the design. Buy-and-hold now loses \$60.29 per
episode on average. It gains \$510.17 in the average up episode, loses
\$710.06 in the average down episode, and roughly breaks even when the
market is flat. Always-buy at \(0.25C_{0}\) behaves the same way at
slightly smaller magnitudes, losing \$58.70 per episode. A policy that
never trades ends every episode with exactly its starting capital, a
wealth change of \$0.00.

On the first study's real test months, buying and holding earned \$180.25
because the period only rose. On the balanced episodes, no fixed pattern
is profitable: constant buying loses money and abstaining earns nothing.
Any agent that earns money here must be reading its observations and
behaving differently in different regimes. The benchmarks are no longer
a wall to climb; they are a test of whether the agent conditions on its
state.

\subsection{Main Results}

Deep Q-learning earned \textbf{+\$126.94 per episode} on average across
the six seeds, with a seed standard deviation of \$7.30. Every other
strategy, learned or fixed, lost money or broke even on the balanced
test set. All six DQN seeds were classified as state-dependent, and all
six were profitable. Table 5.3 summarises the study.

\subsection{The Policy-Gradient Methods Collapse to Corners}

REINFORCE and PPO did not reach that policy reliably. Across the six
REINFORCE seeds, three converged to never trading (a wealth change of
exactly \$0.00), two converged to always buying (\(-\$58.70\), matching
the always-buy row to the cent), and one learned a genuinely
conditioned policy. PPO was similar: five seeds never traded and one
adopted a fixed buying pattern. No PPO seed was state-dependent.

The corners are not senseless. On balanced data, unconditional buying
loses money, so never trading is a locally safe answer: it earns
nothing, but it cannot lose. A policy-gradient method that drifts toward
either deterministic corner stops exploring, because the gradient of a
near-deterministic policy carries almost no signal. The value-based
method does not have this failure mode, since epsilon-greedy exploration
keeps visiting both actions regardless of what the current policy
prefers.

The single conditioned REINFORCE seed matters, though. It earned
\$83.13 on the simulated test, keeping \$417.02 of the up-regime profit
while cutting the down-regime loss to \$162.11. The policy the study
wants is therefore inside REINFORCE's reach; the training process just
does not find it dependably. One seed in six is an existence proof, not
a method.

The comparison so far uses the nine-feature state and the plain
wealth-change reward. The next three sections repeat the first study's
remaining experiments --- the state representation, the risk-aware
reward, and the transaction fee --- under the corrected data.

\section{Effect of the State Representation}

Chapter 3 defines the nine-feature state as the main state
representation and treats drawdown as an additional risk-related
feature. The first study found that adding drawdown changed nothing
measurable, but that finding was suspect for the same reason as every
first-study finding: an agent that memorises 60 months has no use for any
feature. This section asks the question again under the corrected data:
does adding drawdown to the state change performance or behaviour when
the reward stays wealth-change? The comparison keeps everything else
fixed: the same simulated training and test episodes, the same
\(0.25C_{0}\) trade size, the same six seeds. Table 5.4 reports both

For Deep Q-learning, the first study's conclusion survives the correction.
Adding drawdown moves the mean from +\$126.94 to +\$131.31, a change of

than a feature effect. REINFORCE's six ten-feature seeds all traded this

policy its nine-feature run found once), and PPO moved from five
abstaining seeds to five abstaining seeds with one weakly conditioned
run. With seed distributions this bimodal, the mean differences between
the state configurations say more about which corner more seeds fell
into than about the feature. The feature question can only be answered
cleanly by the method whose seeds agree, and for that method the answer
is: no measurable effect under the wealth-change reward.

\section{Effect of the Risk-Aware Reward}

The first study's sharpest negative finding was about the risk-aware reward:
penalising drawdown reduced risk only by reducing participation, and the
Sharpe ratio fell. This section repeats the experiment on the corrected
data, and the conclusion changes. The reward is the one defined in
Chapter 3,

\[r_{t}^{risk} = \Delta W_{t} - \lambda C_{0}\Delta dd_{t},\]

where \(\Delta dd_{t}\) is the increase in portfolio drawdown at trading
day \(t\) (zero when drawdown does not rise) and \(\lambda\) controls
the penalty. It is paired with the ten-feature state, which carries the
drawdown information the penalty refers to.

\subsection{Selection of the Penalty Size}

The penalty weight was selected on the 300 simulated validation
episodes, using Deep Q-learning as the selector and the first study's rule:
the largest \(\lambda\) that keeps at least half of the unpenalised
return and at least half of the unpenalised exposure. The unpenalised
agent earned +\$158.29 at exposure 0.598, so the floors were
\(\Delta W \geq +\$79.14\) and exposure \(\geq 0.299\). Table 5.5 and
Figure 5.3 show the sweep.

market was falling}. This is precisely the selective risk management the
first study found no evidence of.

The first study's conclusion is therefore overturned, and the overturning has
the same explanation as everything else in this chapter. Penalising
drawdown can only produce selective behaviour if the agent's value
estimates can distinguish a falling market from a rising one, and value
estimates trained on 60 months could not. The first study measured what the
penalty does to an agent that cannot see; this study measures what it
does to one that can.

Two caveats keep the result honest. First, the policy-gradient methods
collapse completely under the penalty: every REINFORCE seed and
effectively every PPO seed stops trading, because for an unreliable
policy the penalty makes abstention strictly safer than participation.
The risk-aware reward is only usable by the method whose estimates
support it. Second, the defensiveness costs return on transfer: the
risk-trained Deep Q agents earn +\$36.58 per month on the 180 real
months against +\$58.47 for their wealth-trained counterparts, with a
lower transfer Sharpe (0.156 against 0.218). The real window rose more
often than the balanced simulation assumes, so insurance calibrated on
balanced conditions is over-purchased on real data. The wealth-trained
agent remains the better real-market configuration; the risk-trained one
demonstrates that the reward mechanism works when the data supports it.

because frequent trading can reduce the returns produced by a strategy,
even when individual decisions appear reasonable. The main experiments
assume a fee of 0.05\% per trade, which is defensible for a liquid
instrument like SPY but is still an assumption. A result that only
survives at an optimistic cost is not a result, so the fee was swept
from 0 to 50 basis points on the simulated validation episodes with the

The trading columns explain why. The Deep Q agents make about five
trades per episode, and those trades are regime-selective: entering when
the features say the market is rising, exiting when they say it is
falling. Each trade is worth far more than even a punitive fee, so the
agents keep trading --- the rate falls only from 5.5 to 3.7 --- and keep
most of their profit. This differs from the first study, where DQN cut its
trading 83\% as fees rose. The first study's agent traded on noisy value
estimates, so its trades were marginal and a higher fee tipped them into
unprofitability; this study's agent trades on reliable estimates, so its
trades absorb the fee instead.

The policy-gradient methods respond by leaving. REINFORCE's trading
falls from 6.1 trades per episode to zero by 25 basis points, and PPO's
from 11.3 to 0.3: rising costs make the abstention corner more
attractive, and the corner-seeking dynamics of Section 5.6 finish the
job. Fees, like the balanced data itself, separate the method that
prices its actions from the methods that pick a fixed answer. The final
experiment drops the trained policies onto real market data.

\section{Transfer to Real Markets}

The last question is whether policies trained purely on simulated
episodes survive contact with real data. Each trained policy was
evaluated, unchanged, on the 180-month transfer set of Chapter 4: every
real SPY month from 2006 to 2017 and from 2023 to 2025, excluding only
the 2018--2022 calibration window. No agent, and no tuning decision,
ever saw any of these months. The window includes the 2008 financial
crisis, the 2011 correction, and the 2015--16 drawdowns, so it tests
exactly the conditions the simulated down regime stands in for.

\subsection{Overall Transfer Results}

Table 5.8 reports the transfer test. The Deep Q-learning agents earned

to \$66.32), against +\$80.24 for buy-and-hold. They did so at a mean
exposure of 0.709 against the benchmark's 0.908, with roughly two thirds

something to protect against.

\subsection{Worked Example: The 2008 Crisis}

October 2008 is the worst month in the transfer set. Buy-and-hold lost
\$1,665.24 that month, 16.7\% of its starting capital. All six Deep
Q-learning agents held \textbf{zero exposure for the entire month} and
lost nothing. None of them had ever seen October 2008, or any real
month: their training data was simulated, and their calibration months
(2018--2022) lie a decade later. The agents stepped aside because the
month's volatility features matched the simulated down regime they had
learned to avoid.

The same pattern holds across the crisis. From September 2008 to March
2009, buy-and-hold lost \$3,045.66 in accumulated monthly wealth
changes; the agents lost \$180.34. In September 2008 (the Lehman
collapse) they held 0.08 exposure and lost \$178.73 against the
benchmark's \$894.23; from October through February their exposure was
zero or negligible in every seed.

The cost side of the same behaviour is equally visible. Buy-and-hold
gained \$1,068.28 in the December 2008 rebound and \$1,332.73 in March
2009 when the market turned; the agents, still reading crisis-level
volatility, sat both months out. This is the insurance premium in
concrete form: the agent pays it by missing rebounds, and collects when
the market falls. Over the whole window the premium costs more than the
payouts --- that is why buy-and-hold keeps the higher raw return --- but
the agent takes far less risk to earn what it earns, which is what the
Sharpe comparison summarises. Figure 5.5 shows the whole window: the
agents track the benchmark's rises at reduced scale, and the gap between
the two lines through 2008--09 is where the drawdown advantage is
earned.

\subsection{What Transfers and What Does Not}

The transfer results also measure the domain gap. The generator produces
simpler price paths than the real market, so a policy trained on it
cannot be expected to transfer at full strength~\cite{silver2016}. Three
observations describe the gap. First, profitability transfers: every
Deep Q seed is profitable over 180 real months. Second, the behaviour
transfers: the agents reduce exposure in real declines exactly as they
learned to do in simulated ones. Third, precision does not fully
transfer: the agents' timing around real turning points is imperfect ---
sitting out the December 2008 and March 2009 rebounds is the clearest
case --- because real regime changes announce themselves less cleanly
than the generator's fixed regimes.

The algorithm ordering on real data matches the simulated ordering
(Deep Q, then REINFORCE, then PPO), which is further evidence that the
simulated results measure something the real market confirms. The next
section discusses what the two studies establish together.

\section{Discussion of the Main Findings}

The experiments answer the five research questions of Section 5.1. This
section takes each in turn, using the first study and the main study together,
because on three of the five questions the first study alone would have given
the wrong answer.

\subsection{Performance: The Answer Depends on the Measure}

On raw return against buy-and-hold, the learned agents lose on real
data. In the first study, the best agent finished \$8.74 per month behind the
benchmark on the 2024--2025 months. On the 180-month transfer set, the
Deep Q agents earned +\$60.28 per month against buy-and-hold's +\$80.24.
A passive index position over a period that mostly rises is very hard to
outrun, which is consistent with the market-efficiency expectation set
out in Chapter 2~\cite{fama1970}.

On risk-adjusted return, the main study's agents win. Their transfer
Sharpe ratio of 0.224 exceeds buy-and-hold's 0.198, with five of six
seeds at or above the benchmark, because the agents avoid most of the
benchmark's worst losses at a modest cost in upside. And on the balanced
simulated test, where no fixed pattern is profitable, Deep Q-learning is
the only strategy of any kind that makes money (+\$126.94 per episode).
The performance answer is therefore conditional: the agents do not beat
the market's average direction, but they earn their return with
measurably less risk, and they demonstrably possess the
state-conditional skill that the raw-return comparison obscures.

\subsection{Behaviour: The Ranking Measures the Data, Not the
Algorithms}

All three methods condition on the same state, maximise the same reward,
and see the same data, so the first study's behavioural split --- six of six
Deep Q seeds state-dependent, none of twelve policy-gradient seeds ---
cannot mean that one method ``uses the state'' and the others cannot.
The difference lies in what each method must estimate. A value-based
method estimates the value of every state it acts in, which takes many
visits to similar states under a stable distribution; the first study's 60
shifting months could not support those estimates, so Deep Q-learning
conditioned on noise and earned the least. The policy-gradient methods
only need a policy that scores well on the training months, and on
upward-leaning data the always-buy answer is available at zero cost.

The main study confirms this account by inverting the ranking. Given
3,000 balanced episodes, the value estimates become reliable and Deep
Q-learning wins on every seed, while the policy-gradient methods lose
their free answer and collapse to corners: across all main-study
configurations, their seeds land overwhelmingly on never-trade or
fixed-buying policies. The single conditioned REINFORCE seed shows the
optimum is reachable by policy-gradient training but not reliably found
by it, at least not at these settings. The ranking on either dataset
alone would mislead; together, the two rankings identify data volume and
balance as the deciding variable.

\subsection{State Information Matters Only When the Objective Uses It}

Adding the drawdown feature under the wealth-change reward changed
nothing measurable in either study. Adding the same feature under a
reward that penalises drawdown produced selective downside protection
and a higher Sharpe ratio. The pair of results makes a single principle
concrete: information in the state earns its place only when the
learning objective gives the agent a reason to read it. Feature
engineering and objective design are not separate questions.

\subsection{The Risk-Aware Reward Works When the Estimates Do}

The first study concluded that the drawdown penalty was a participation tax.
The main study shows that with reliable value estimates the same penalty
produces exactly the intended behaviour: a 73\% smaller down-regime loss
at a 1.5\% cost in mean return. Both studies agree on the failure mode
--- past a threshold weight, abstention becomes the best reply and
exposure collapses --- but the corrected study locates the threshold
further out and shows genuine risk management below it. The remaining
weakness is calibration across domains: insurance priced on balanced
simulated conditions is over-purchased on real markets that mostly rise,
which is why the wealth-trained agents transfer better than the
risk-trained ones.

\subsection{Costs Test the Nature of the Learned Edge}

The fee sweep separates marginal edges from robust ones. The first study's
agents, trading on noisy estimates, lost most of their activity and
profit as fees rose. The main study's Deep Q agents remain profitable at
a fee ten times the headline assumption, because regime-selective trades
are worth far more than they cost. An edge that survives a tenfold cost
increase is unlikely to be an artefact of the cost assumption.

\subsection{Seed Consistency Is a Result, Not a Nuisance}

The clearest single diagnostic across both studies is the across-seed
spread. Deep Q-learning's main-study seed standard deviation is \$7.30
on a mean of +\$126.94; REINFORCE's is \$52.17 on a mean near zero,
because its seeds split between corners. A method whose six runs agree
is reporting a property of the method; a method whose runs disagree is
reporting a lottery. Single-seed evaluations, still common in the
applied literature reviewed in Chapter 2, could have reported the lucky
REINFORCE seed (+\$83.13) as a successful trading system.

\subsection{Implications for the Research Objectives}

Overall, the two studies together answer the main research question of
whether reinforcement learning algorithms can learn profitable,
state-dependent trading strategies for a portfolio.

On real data at the volumes a single asset provides, they cannot. Sixty
training months support memorisation, not learning, and the agents
either memorised the majority answer or conditioned on value estimates
too noisy to pay.

Given enough balanced data, they can. Deep Q-learning earned money on a
test set where every fixed strategy loses, did so on all six seeds,
behaved correctly in each controlled regime, kept its edge under
punitive transaction costs, used the risk-aware reward as designed, and
transferred its behaviour to two decades of real months --- including
stepping fully out of the market through the worst of the 2008 crisis
--- with a better risk-adjusted return than buy-and-hold. The capability
was never absent from the method. It was absent from the data. The
binding constraint in this problem was not the reward function, the
state design, or the trade size, although each mattered at the margin;
it was the amount and balance of training data, and once that constraint
was lifted, the remaining differences between algorithms were
differences in how they explore and what they must estimate.

\section{Chapter Summary}

The chapter reported two studies. The first study trained the
agents on the 60 real training months and tested them on 2024--2025: no
agent beat buy-and-hold, the policy-gradient methods ignored their
observations, and the one state-dependent method earned the least. The
diagnosis attributed all of it to data: 60 episodes cannot train
networks of 18,000 parameters beyond memorisation, and an upward-leaning
market makes constant buying an unbeatable shortcut.

The main study removed those conditions and re-ran everything. On 3,000
balanced simulated episodes with a 2,800-parameter network, Deep
Q-learning earned +\$126.94 per episode on a test set where no fixed
trading pattern is profitable, on all six seeds, capturing 99\% of the
rising-market profit and cutting the falling-market loss by 84\%. The
trade-size rule re-selected \(0.25C_{0}\); the drawdown feature again
changed nothing under the wealth objective; the risk-aware reward, which
had only reduced participation in the first study, now cut down-regime losses
by 73\% at almost no cost in return; and the learned edge survived a
tenfold fee increase. Transferred unchanged to 180 real months, the
policies stayed profitable on every seed, held zero exposure through
October 2008, and finished with a higher Sharpe ratio than buy-and-hold.
The policy-gradient methods collapsed to fixed corner policies
throughout. The conclusion that survives both studies is that the data,
not the algorithms, set the limit on what was learnable --- and that
value-based learning with sustained exploration was the only method able
to exploit the data once it was available. Chapter 6 draws the
conclusions.
