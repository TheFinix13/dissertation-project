\section{Summary of the Work}

This dissertation examined whether reinforcement learning algorithms can
learn profitable trading policies that utilize market and portfolio
information from the observed states when trading assets in a portfolio.

The study designed a complete experimental system for trading the SPY
exchange-traded fund. The system uses daily market data organised into
monthly episodes, includes transaction costs and action masking, and
uses a nine-feature state representation with an optional drawdown
feature. Two reward formulations were tested: the primary wealth-change
reward and an optional risk-aware reward that penalised drawdown, along
with three learning algorithms: REINFORCE, Deep Q-learning (DQN), and
Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO).

Experimental decisions were made on validation data throughout: the
12-month 2023 dataset in the first study, and 300 simulated validation
episodes in the main study. Test data, real or simulated, was used only
for final evaluations.

The experiments followed a fixed sequence. First, the trade size was
adjusted to ensure the agents could reach a reasonable level of exposure
relative to the buy-and-hold benchmark. The behavioural experiment then
determined whether the learned policies actually respond to the
information in their states. Once these environment, calibration and
behavioural choices had been decided, the main performance of the
learned agents was evaluated and compared against the benchmark strategy
on the unseen test period.

Additional experiments then tested whether adding drawdown to the state
changed the results, whether a risk-aware reward improved loss
management in the portfolio trade, and how the different learning
methods responded to changes in transaction costs.

The real-data results then motivated a second study. The failures of the
first were diagnosed as a data problem: 60 training episodes cannot
train networks of 18,000 parameters to do anything except memorise. A
market simulator with three controlled conditions --- up, down, and flat
--- was calibrated on the training years,
3,000 balanced episodes were generated, the network was reduced to about
2,800 parameters, and all three algorithms were retrained and evaluated
under controlled rising, falling, and flat conditions. The trained
policies were then transferred, unchanged, to 180 real months that no
training or tuning step ever read --- 2006 to 2017 and 2023 to 2025,
including the 2008 financial crisis.

\section{Conclusions Against the Research Questions}

The experiments in Chapter 5 address the five questions asked in 5.1.

\subsection{Performance}

The first question considered whether the reinforcement learning agents
could outperform the buy-and-hold benchmark on unseen data. The answer
depends on which data, and on which measure.

The main study gives the positive half of the answer. On the balanced
simulated test set, where buy-and-hold loses \$60.29 per episode, Deep
Q-learning earned +\$126.94 and was the only strategy of any kind to
make money. On the 180-month real transfer set, buy-and-hold kept the
higher raw return (+\$80.24 against +\$60.28 per month), but the Deep Q
agents earned it at three-quarters of the exposure and two-thirds of the
drawdown, and their mean Sharpe ratio of 0.224 exceeded the benchmark's
0.198. On raw return over a rising market, buy-and-hold remains
unbeaten; on risk-adjusted return over a window containing real crashes,
the learned agents finished ahead.

The first study supplies the negative half. Trained on the 60 real
months alone, no learned agent outperformed buy-and-hold: the benchmark
produced a mean wealth change of +\$180.25 per month on the 2024--2025
test months, while the best learned agent, REINFORCE, achieved
+\$171.51. PPO achieved +\$127.35 and DQN +\$74.68. The test period
favoured remaining invested, with positive market performance in 17 of
the 24 months, so buy-and-hold was difficult to beat.

Transaction costs and the trade size cannot explain these differences,
since they were the same throughout the experiments, providing a basis
for fair comparison.

\subsection{Behaviour}

The second question examined whether the learned policies genuinely use
the state information provided to them.

The behavioural split is the finding on which the two studies agree most
strongly. In the main study, all six Deep Q-learning seeds were
state-dependent and earned +\$126.94 per episode, while the
policy-gradient seeds overwhelmingly collapsed to fixed corner policies
--- never trading, or buying constantly --- once the balanced data
removed the free always-buy answer. In the first study, the same split
appeared without the reward: all six DQN seeds changed their actions in
response to the observed state, while none of the twelve policy-gradient
seeds from REINFORCE and PPO did so.

The first study shows why the analysis matters. REINFORCE came close to
the benchmark in terms of return, at +\$171.51 per month, but its
policies largely shifted to buying rather than using the market
information provided. An agent can perform well for the wrong reason:
during a generally rising market, a policy that remains invested can
achieve a strong return without needing to learn when market conditions
require entering, reducing, or exiting a position. DQN produced policies
that consistently responded to the state yet earned the least, so state
dependence and financial performance were not the same thing at that
data volume. The main study resolves the tension: once the data could
support reliable value estimates, the state-dependent method was also
the profitable one, on every seed.

Looking only at return would have completely hidden the distinction
between these behaviours. The behavioural analysis is therefore an
important part of evaluating learned trading policies, as it revealed a
clear difference between the value-based and policy-gradient methods.

\subsection{State Representation}

The third question examined whether adding a drawdown feature to the
state representation improved agent performance or behaviour.

Both studies give the same answer, and the main study gives it cleanly.
In the first study, adding drawdown changed each algorithm's mean return by
less than its seed variation. In the main study, where the seeds agree
and the comparison is therefore meaningful, Deep Q-learning moved from
+\$126.94 to +\$131.31 per episode --- a +\$4.37 change against a seed
standard deviation near \$7 --- with identical behaviour on every
measure. The extra information neither helped nor hurt while the
objective remained plain wealth change.

Overall, the results show that providing additional information does
not automatically produce a better policy or improve learning unless the
objective specifies it. This is consistent with an important principle
noticed from the experiments: \textbf{information in the state is only
useful when the learning objective and optimisation process give the
agent a reason to use it}. The risk-aware reward experiment below is the
positive half of the same principle: once the objective referred to
drawdown, the drawdown feature became useful.

\subsection{Reward Formulation}

The fourth question considered whether adding drawdown into the reward
could encourage the agents to reduce losses and generate a more
risk-aware behaviour. This is the question on which the two studies
disagree, and the reason they disagree matters as much as the answer.

In the first study, the risk-aware reward reduced drawdown mainly by reducing
market participation. With the selected penalty of \(\lambda = 0.10\),
DQN\textquotesingle s drawdown fell 41\% but its return fell 32\%, its
exposure halved, and its Sharpe ratio dropped from 0.505 to 0.416. The
reward appeared to tax participation rather than teach risk management.

The main study overturns that conclusion. With \(\lambda = 0.25\),
selected on the simulated validation episodes by the same rule, Deep
Q-learning kept its return almost unchanged (+\$129.38 against +\$131.31
per episode) while cutting drawdown by 31\% and raising its Sharpe ratio
from 0.285 to 0.332. The results for each market condition show the improvement is
selective, not proportional: the down-market loss fell 73\% while the
up-market gain kept 87\% of its size. The agent reduced its holdings
when the market fell, which is exactly the behaviour the reward was
designed to produce.

The first study's negative finding was therefore another data artefact. A
drawdown penalty can only produce selective behaviour if the value
estimates behind the policy can tell a falling market from a rising one,
and estimates trained on 60 months could not. Two qualifications remain.
The penalty pushed every policy-gradient seed into complete abstention,
so the mechanism is only usable by a method with reliable estimates and
sustained exploration. And the risk-trained agents transfer more
conservatively than the real market rewards: on the 180 real months they
earned +\$36.58 per month against +\$58.47 for the wealth-trained
agents, so the wealth-trained configuration remains the better
real-market choice.

\subsection{Data}

The fifth question asked whether the amount and balance of the training
data explain the learned behaviour, and whether fixing the data fixes
the behaviour. The simulated-data study answers yes to both.

On 3,000 balanced simulated episodes, Deep Q-learning earned +\$126.94
per episode across six seeds (standard deviation \$7.30) on a test set
where buy-and-hold loses \$60.29 and doing nothing earns \$0.00. All six
seeds were profitable and all six were state-dependent. The results in
each market condition show the behaviour the real-data study looked for: the agents
captured 99\% of the profit available in rising episodes and cut the
falling-episode loss by 84\% relative to always buying. The same
policies remained profitable on every seed when transferred unchanged to
the 180 real transfer months, and the defensive behaviour they learned
in simulation appeared where it mattered: all six agents held zero
exposure through October 2008, the month buy-and-hold lost \$1,665.24,
and across the seven crisis months from September 2008 to March 2009
they lost \$180.34 where the benchmark lost \$3,045.66.

The study also explains the real-data algorithm ranking. Deep
Q-learning was worst on real data not because it is unsuited to trading
but because value estimation needs many visits to similar states, which
60 shifting months cannot provide. Once the data supported the
estimates, the ranking inverted. REINFORCE and PPO, which looked strong
on real data by adopting the majority always-buy action, collapsed to
fixed corner policies on balanced data, where no fixed action is
profitable. One REINFORCE seed in six did learn the conditioned policy,
so the optimum is within reach of policy-gradient methods, but the
training process does not find it reliably.

The overall conclusion of the dissertation therefore has two halves. At
real single-asset data volumes, none of the tested algorithms learns a
genuine trading policy. With enough balanced data, value-based learning
does, on every seed, and behaves sensibly in every market condition the
simulator can produce.

\section{Critical Appraisal of the Project}

The most important lessons from this project were from the experimental
methodology rather than the choice of reinforcement learning algorithm
alone. Several of the most useful findings came from implementation
problems identified during development and testing.

One of the strongest aspects of the project was the verification tests.
The automated tests identified several implementation problems during
development, \textbf{three of which could have affected the
experiments' validity}. These
included issues with rolling feature calculation at the end of monthly
episodes and an implementation error in the buy-and-hold benchmark that
reduced its performance by \$38.95 per month, about 21.6\%. If this
error had remained, the dissertation\textquotesingle s main conclusion
could have been misleading, as the learned agents would have appeared to
outperform the benchmark.

The verification tests also noticed the experimental design problem in
the trade size. Using the original \(0.10C_{0}\) trade size in Chapter 3
would make the maximum attainable exposure only 0.690, compared with
0.910 for buy-and-hold. This meant that the learning agents were at a
disadvantage before training even began. Selecting \(0.25C_{0}\) allowed
a fairer comparison while still letting the agents control their
positions in smaller steps than the\(0.50C_{0}\) alternative.

The complete set of 21 verification tests passed before the final
experiments were accepted, giving a higher confidence that the reported
results reflect an appropriate experimental design rather than
implementation errors.

The behavioural analysis was another important part of the study.
Without it, the REINFORCE result of +\$171.51 could have been
interpreted as evidence that the agent had learned an intelligent,
effective trading strategy. The analysis showed this interpretation was
not justified, as the policies acted like a consistent buyer because the
market was consistently rising, completely changing the interpretation
of the result.

The project also highlighted the importance of random seed variation.
The results, particularly for PPO, varied considerably between
independent training runs. PPO had a seed spread of \$179.80 in one
configuration, which was greater than its mean return. This made it
difficult to draw conclusions based on individual training runs, as that
would have been unreliable. Reporting multiple seeds widened the
evaluation, providing a more consistent view of how each algorithm
behaved, although it increased the computational and experimental
workload; however, six seeds are still lacking for more definite
statistical inference.

The largest single lesson, however, was about data. The project spent
most of its effort on the state representation, the reward, the trade
size, and the verification of the environment, and all of that effort
was necessary; none of it was sufficient. The binding constraint was 60
training episodes feeding 18,000 parameters, and no amount of feature or
reward engineering could compensate for it. Diagnosing that constraint
and rebuilding the study around simulated data changed the results more
than every other design decision combined. The lesson for similar
projects is to budget the data before budgeting the model: count the
episodes, count the parameters, and if the second number is larger,
change the data or the model before running anything else.

Overall, the project demonstrated that careful experimental design is as
important as the choice of reinforcement learning algorithm. The main
lessons came from testing the assumptions behind the experiments, rather
than from simply comparing model returns. State dependence, action
constraints, reward design, seed variation, and above all data volume
affect how the final results could be interpreted. This made the
experimental process more demanding but also produced a more reliable
basis for evaluating the learned trading policies.

\section{Limitations}

The findings of this study should be considered within the scope of the
experimental design, but there are several limitations.

\subsection{One asset}

The experiments use SPY as the only asset. The main study's transfer set
does cover a wide range of real market conditions, including the 2008
crisis, so the earlier concern that no real declining period was tested
no longer applies. What remains untested is breadth across instruments:
a single, highly liquid index fund cannot show how the approach behaves
on individual stocks, other asset classes, or portfolios of several
assets, where correlations and idiosyncratic events matter. The findings
cannot therefore be automatically generalised beyond broad index trading
without further testing.

\subsection{Simulator realism and the domain gap}

The market generator draws each day's return independently within a
condition. Real returns are not independent: volatility clusters, trends
persist, and market conditions change mid-month. The generator also holds
one condition for a whole episode, so the agents never face a mid-month
crash.
These simplifications are what make the controlled experiment possible,
and the transfer test measures their cost directly: the Deep Q-learning agents
kept roughly three-quarters of buy-and-hold's return over the 180 real
transfer months while being trained on simulated data alone, and their
timing around real turning points was imperfect --- they sat out the
December 2008 and March 2009 rebounds because real markets change
condition less cleanly than the generator's fixed ones.
Conclusions about absolute profitability on real markets should
therefore rest on the transfer numbers, not the simulated ones.

\subsection{Policy-gradient training was not separately tuned}

REINFORCE and PPO collapsed to corner policies on the balanced simulated
data. Both were trained with the same hyperparameters as the real-data
study, including PPO's default entropy coefficient of zero. Entropy
regularisation or other exploration incentives might let the
policy-gradient methods find the conditioned policy more reliably, as
one REINFORCE seed shows it exists. The study reports the collapse as
observed behaviour under shared settings rather than as a bound on what
tuned policy-gradient methods could achieve.

\subsection{Monthly Episode Structure}

The trading environment treated each calendar month as a separate
episode. Every episode started with the same initial capital of
\$10,000, and any remaining position was closed at the end of the month.
As a result, the results do not represent a single portfolio that
continuously compounds over the full evaluation period as a real
portfolio would.

This design made the experiments easier to control by keeping the
episode length and starting conditions consistent. However, it also
means that gains or losses from one month did not affect the starting
capital or portfolio position in the following month, which does not
represent a real-world portfolio. A longer continuous portfolio
environment could therefore lead to a different behaviour, especially
for policies that require accumulated wealth or changes in portfolio
value over time.

\subsection{Fixed Trade Size}

The agents trade fixed fractions of the initial capital rather than
choosing their position size continuously. This was calibrated to create
a controlled, reasonable environment for comparison with the benchmark,
but it restricts how much the agents can express their decisions. A
continuous position-sizing model could potentially respond differently
based on its exposure in a particular market condition. Rather than
repeatedly applying fixed buy or sell actions, such an agent could
select its desired portfolio exposure, producing different results in
returns and trading behaviour.

\subsection{Limited Number of Seeds and Lack of Significance Testing}

Six seeds were used for each configuration, and the seed-level results
and variation between them are reported throughout the results. This is
stronger than relying on a single training run, but it does not provide
sufficient statistics for formal claims about small differences. For
example, the +\$4.37 change observed for Deep Q-learning when drawdown
was added to the state sits within a seed standard deviation of about
\$7 and is not large enough to be treated as a meaningful improvement.

\subsection{Risk Penalty Selection was based on DQN}

The reward\textquotesingle s penalty weight λ was selected using DQN
because it was the algorithm that consistently showed state-dependent
behaviour. This does not guarantee that the same penalty value is
appropriate for REINFORCE or PPO, as it was not independently optimised
for every algorithm. A separate calibration for each algorithm could
produce different results. This limitation is particularly relevant
because the algorithms responded differently to the state and reward
formulation.

\subsection{The risk penalty only targets drawdown}

The risk-aware reward penalises drawdown but does not directly measure
whether the return achieved is sufficient for the level of risk taken.
While drawdown is directly relevant to losses, it does not fully
represent the portfolio risk. A reward that relates return to risk,
rather than penalizing risk alone, was not tested.

The main study shows the penalty working as intended at the selected
weight, but the sweep in Section 5.8 also shows the failure mode is
still there: one grid step past the selected weight, exposure collapses
and the agents leave the market. The penalty's usefulness therefore
depends on a calibration that the study performed on validation data; a
reward whose optimum did not sit next to a collapse point would be more
robust.

\section{Future Work}

The limitations above suggest several directions for furthering the
research. Each stems directly from a limitation or an unresolved result
in the experiments. The behavioural analysis should also be applied to
these future works to determine the agent's behaviour to changes
alongside performance improvements.

\subsection{Realistic Market Generators}

The simulated-data study used the simplest generator that supports a
controlled experiment. The natural next step is a generator that keeps
the control but adds the structure the current one omits: changes of
market condition within an episode, driven by a hidden Markov chain, and
volatility clustering within a condition. Learned generators such as
time-series GANs offer more realism again, at the cost of transparency.
Each step up in realism should shrink the domain gap that the transfer
test measures, and the transfer test itself provides the yardstick for
whether it does.

\subsection{Walk-Forward Evaluation and Other Markets}

The transfer test now covers two decades of real months, including the
2008 crisis, but it evaluates fixed policies calibrated once on
2018--2022. A walk-forward design, in which the simulator is
recalibrated and the agents retrained as conditions change, would test
whether the approach holds up as an operating procedure rather than as
a one-shot experiment. Extending the evaluation to other index funds,
individual stocks, or non-equity assets would test whether the learned
response to market conditions is specific to SPY's statistics or carries
across markets.

\subsection{Predictive and Uncertainty-Aware State Information}

The drawdown feature describes the portfolio\textquotesingle s previous
losses but does not provide information about future market movements.
Future work could therefore include predictive information, such as
probabilistic forecasts containing estimates of forecast uncertainty.

This would give a state-dependent agent more useful information for
making decisions about future market conditions rather than relying only
on historical data. It would also directly test whether
DQN\textquotesingle s ability to use the state leads to more useful
market understanding and trading decisions.

\subsection{Risk-Efficiency Objectives}

The most direct refinement of the drawdown penalty would be a reward
that considers return relative to risk, such as a Sharpe-based or
drawdown-relative objective~\cite{moody2001}. Two results motivate
this. The penalty sweep in Section 5.8 shows a collapse one step past
the selected weight, where reducing exposure to zero becomes the best
reply;
a risk-relative objective would remove abstention as a way of scoring
well. And the transfer results show the risk-trained agents giving up
more return for downside protection than the real market's declines
justify; an objective that weighs risk against return, rather than
penalising risk alone, should calibrate that trade-off automatically.

\subsection{More Seeds for Better Statistical Analysis}

More random training seeds would improve the reliability of comparisons~\cite{henderson2018}
between algorithms and configurations\textbf{.} Future work should
combine this with appropriate statistical analysis to determine whether
observed differences between configurations are likely to represent
genuine results rather than random noise.

\subsection{More flexible trade sizes}

Future experiments could allow the agent to choose its position size
continuously rather than using fixed trade amounts. This would remove
the exposure limit created by the current environment and allow the
agent to express different levels of confidence by adjusting its
position size, potentially enabling smoother portfolio adjustments.

\section{Closing Remarks}

This dissertation examined whether standard reinforcement learning could
learn a genuine trading policy for a single asset. On real data it could
not: the best-performing learned agent finished \$8.74 per month below
buy-and-hold, and it did so by ignoring its market observations to
follow a simple trading behaviour.

The study's contribution is what it did with that failure. The
behavioural analysis showed the strong-looking returns were not evidence
of learning; the diagnosis traced the failure to 60 training episodes
feeding 18,000 parameters; and the main study removed that constraint
and showed the same algorithms succeeding. Deep Q-learning became
profitable and state-dependent on every seed, and its policies, trained
on simulated prices alone, stayed profitable across 180 real months they
had never seen --- stepping fully out of the market through October
2008 and finishing the two decades with a better risk-adjusted return
than buy-and-hold. The negative result and the positive one are the same
finding viewed from two sides: what these methods learn is decided by
the data they are given.

The implementation, the market simulator, the experimental framework and
the behavioural analysis developed in this study provide a useful basis
for future work. More advanced models and more realistic generators can build on
this framework while being evaluated against the same fair standards.
